# Research abstract

**Physics-Informed Machine Learning for Anomaly Detection and Candidate Transit Identification in TESS Light Curves**

Photometric observations from the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) provide a large search space for identifying transit-like signals and unusual stellar variability. Conventional transit-search algorithms such as Box Least Squares (BLS) provide established detection statistics, while machine-learning approaches offer additional ways to rank unusual light curves for further investigation.

This work investigates a physics-informed machine-learning framework for identifying and characterising anomalous transit-like signatures in publicly available TESS light curves. Here, physics-informed refers to astrophysically motivated features, including transit depth, duration, periodicity, morphology, variability, and an approximate transit-depth radius ratio, rather than physical equations enforced during model training. The pipeline performs quality filtering, normalisation, detrending, and segmentation, followed by a BLS search and feature extraction. Stellar-catalogue-dependent radius and duration-consistency proxies are calculated where measurements permit, but are excluded from default model inputs because their availability differs between reference groups.

The resulting representations are analysed using unsupervised anomaly-detection methods, including Isolation Forest and Local Outlier Factor, to identify light curves that receive high anomaly scores relative to the analysed feature-space population. Known exoplanet systems, planet candidates, and catalogue-labelled false-positive systems provide reference populations for evaluating whether anomaly scores add ranking information beyond conventional BLS statistics. Catalogue labels are used only for evaluation. The primary experiment fits and ranks the same unlabelled sample, so its results are descriptive rather than an independent evaluation of generalisation.

Interpretability is investigated through feature-score associations, feature-family ablations, principal-component visualisation, and individual light-curve diagnostic reports. These analyses examine properties associated with high anomaly scores and their correspondence to physically meaningful transit characteristics. They do not establish causation, provide formal per-object model attribution, or calibrate anomaly scores as statistical significance.

The study implements and evaluates a reproducible framework in which machine learning acts as a candidate-prioritisation layer alongside established astronomical techniques. It asks whether anomaly-based or hybrid rankings improve known-system recovery at fixed inspection budgets. Interpretation accounts for catalogue periods outside the search interval and unequal sector coverage between reference populations; highly ranked unlabelled backgrounds are inspection candidates, not validated planets. Whether this approach reduces manual inspection workload while retaining useful candidates remains a research question requiring independent evaluation and subsequent astrophysical validation.
